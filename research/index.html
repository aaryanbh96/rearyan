Aryan Bhardwaj Projects

How do automated and AI-enabled operational systems change human judgment, coordination, and error propagation inside organizations?

The question came from building and deploying such systems in an operating company, and watching what changed around them.

Where it came from

Since early 2025 I have worked as a Data & Growth Consultant for a specialty tea manufacturer in Portland that sells into retail. I built six production systems because we had operational problems: purchase orders took forty minutes to check by hand, cartons were planned from memory, the product catalog lived in spreadsheets. The systems worked. What interested me was what happened after they did.

Routine checks moved from people into software, and the people moved from performing the checks to supervising the systems that performed them. Separately, I caught a single wrong product identifier that had propagated, unnoticed, through retailer compliance systems into thousands of dollars of deductions. Putting the two side by side, I could not say whether moving checks into software changes anyone's attention, whether the habit of looking erodes, or how I would know. That is the question: when routine judgment moves into systems, what happens to the judgment, the coordination, and the errors that remain for people to catch, and under what conditions?

Before this I spent five years as a human-in-the-loop evaluator for large-scale machine-learning systems, rating outputs against detailed guidelines. That is the same question from the other side: what human judgment looks like when it is a component inside an automated pipeline.

These observations connect to long-standing questions about the ironies of automation (Bainbridge, 1983), appropriate reliance on automation (Lee & See, 2004), distributed cognition (Hutchins, 1995), and how technologies and organizational routines reshape one another (Leonardi, 2011). I am particularly interested in whether moving routine verification into software changes how workers detect and recover from errors. One approach would combine field observation and interviews with operational trace data, comparing verification practices across workflows. Whether any change reflects reduced vigilance, shifted responsibility, or simply better task allocation is an open question that needs systematic evidence.

Six systems, and what changed around them

System What it replaced What I observed afterward
Purchase-order verification About forty minutes of line-by-line checking per order against the product master and retailer rules. A language model extracts the fields; deterministic code does the matching, arithmetic, and rule checks. On a real 71-line order: 71 lines extracted, 69 matched, 2 absent products flagged rather than guessed. Checking takes under two minutes. Staff review flagged exceptions instead of reading every line.
Demand planning Ordering from memory and recent sales. Baseline-versus-promotion tracking measures lift per retailer. A new question appeared that did not exist before: when should a person override the forecast, and on what grounds?
Carton packing Hand-planned cartons. Weight-ceiling bin-packing chooses the configuration. Adopted daily by non-technical staff. The planning step disappeared from the job.
Catalog and inventory Spreadsheets as the source of truth. A database system enforces integrity rules at the point of entry. One record now feeds every downstream document. Whatever it says, right or wrong, is what everything else says.
Labels Retyping product data onto retailer labels. Retyping is gone, and with it one point where a person would have reread the data. A wrong identifier can now reach every label without a manual check.
Packing slips Documents assembled by hand from the order. Fewer hands touch each document between order and shipment.

These are observations from running the operation, not measured outcomes. Whether attention, expertise, or responsibility actually changed, and under what conditions, is exactly what I don't yet know. Making it knowable is the research.
